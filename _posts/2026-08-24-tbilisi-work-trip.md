I just got back from a work trip to Tbilisi, Georgia. It was, by a
comfortable margin, the best trip I've taken in years, and also the one
where I spent thirty-six hours dressed entirely in merchandise from a
single Levi's store. Both of those things are true. Let me explain.

As always, the shorter, in-the-moment version — photos, quick takes —
lives on Mastodon at **[@w0ger@woof.group](https://woof.group/@w0ger)**.

## The Journey Out

The flight left Minneapolis about three hours late. This was a Delta
ticket operated by Air France, which is a sentence that will become
relevant several times before this post is over.

Business class dinner was salmon. I want to be clear that nobody forced
me. I looked at the menu, I saw the word "salmon," and I — a person who
has seen *Airplane!* more than once and understood its central lesson —
ordered the fish. Fifteen hundred people have died on screen for this
exact mistake and I walked right into it. It was the worst business class
meal I've ever had. I survived, which I mention only because for a while
it was in question.

Then Charles de Gaulle. I had a two-hour layover and needed to get from
Terminal 2E to Terminal 2D, which sounds like walking down a hallway and
is instead a small expedition involving passport control, an indeterminate
corridor, and a second security screening where I once again explained my
toiletries to a stranger. CDG is not so much an airport as several
buildings that resent each other and refuse to speak.

I ate in 2E. This turned out to be the single best decision of the entire
trip, because Terminal 2D's dining situation is a sandwich kiosk and a
prayer.

## The Bag Takes a Detour

I landed in Tbilisi. My luggage did not.

Somewhere between Paris and Georgia, my bag decided that a two-hour
layover was insufficient and extended its stay. Bold. Frankly the kind of
decisive travel planning I aspire to. I filed the report at the baggage
desk and went to the hotel with a carry-on, a phone charger, and the
clothes I had eaten the salmon in.

The next morning, one of the guys from work took one look at me, said
nothing about it, and drove me to a Levi's. I bought a personality off the
rack — denim from waist to ankle, purchased under fluorescent lighting, in
a country I had landed in the day before. It's a small thing and it was
also the kindest thing anyone did for me all week. If you travel for work
long enough you learn that the difference between a bad trip and a fine
one is usually one colleague noticing something and not making a big deal
out of it.

A few days later the airline emailed to say my luggage had been booked on
its own flight. It had a flight number. It had an itinerary. It arrived in
Tbilisi on a direct flight with a confirmed seat, while I had gotten there
via a three-hour delay and a piece of fish. I'd like to speak with whoever
booked its trip.

## The City

Here's the part I actually want to talk about.

Tbilisi is a wonderful city. Genuinely. It's built into hills, so there's
a view down a street in nearly every direction, and the old town is all
wooden balconies stacked on top of each other in a way that shouldn't work
structurally and clearly has been working for a very long time. We walked
Old Tbilisi on my last morning and I kept stopping to look at things,
which is not something I usually do.

## Mtskheta

We took a day for sightseeing and went out to Mtskheta, northwest of the
city, and this is where the trip stopped being a work trip.

**Jvari Monastery** sits on a hilltop above the confluence of two rivers.
It was built in the sixth century. Not restored in the sixth century —
built. It's been standing on that hill, looking entirely unbothered, for
roughly fifteen hundred years, and you can walk into it. It's a UNESCO
World Heritage site along with the other historic structures of Mtskheta,
and standing up there I had one of those moments where your sense of what
counts as an "old building" gets quietly recalibrated. My house was built
in a decade I can name.

**Svetitskhoveli Cathedral** is down in the town itself. Early Middle
Ages, also UNESCO-listed, currently the second largest church building in
Georgia after Holy Trinity Cathedral in Tbilisi. Photos do not do it. It
is enormous in a way that's hard to convey and the interior has a weight
to it that I'm not going to be able to describe well, so I'll stop trying.

I ranked these below my luggage arriving in a post at the time. That was a
joke. Mostly.

## The Long Way Home

The car came at 4:30 in the morning. Three flights — Tbilisi to London,
London to Dallas, Dallas to Minneapolis — and I walked in my front door at
11:00 PM Central the same calendar day. Georgia runs nine hours ahead of
Central time, so that "same day" was actually 27.5 hours door to door. On
a clock it looks like eighteen. It is not eighteen.

Two good things happened on the way back.

The first: I'd put an AirTag in my bag before leaving Tbilisi, having
learned something this week about object permanence. I could confirm at
every stop that my luggage and I were still in the same country. This is a
small technological convenience that delivered a completely
disproportionate amount of peace of mind, and I'm never traveling without
one again.

The second: I spent part of the London leg having an actual conversation
with a Russian woman flying to Miami to visit her daughter, conducted
entirely through Apple's translate app with a phone held between us. Two
strangers, no shared vocabulary, one very patient piece of software. I
understood roughly none of the words and all of the meaning. Best twenty
minutes of the trip home.

Oh — and I mentioned to a flight attendant that I'd never been on an A380
before, and she gave me a tour of the flight deck. I would like to state
for the record that I touched nothing. There are approximately four
thousand switches up there and every single one of them looks important. I
sat very still, said "wow" several times, and kept my hands visible at all
times.

## Would Recommend

Final tally: three flights home, 27.5 hours door to door, one bag that
traveled independently for part of the week, two margaritas, one flight
deck tour, two UNESCO World Heritage sites, one pair of jeans I now have
real affection for, and one piece of salmon I will never fully forgive.

Tbilisi was wonderful. The work went well, the city is beautiful, the food
is excellent, and the people I traveled with were the kind of people you
want to be stuck somewhere with.

Go if you get the chance. Pack a carry-on.
